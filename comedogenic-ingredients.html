Comedogenic ingredients to avoid on oily skin

A quick guide to the comedogenic scale, and the specific ingredients most likely to clog pores if you're oily or acne-prone.

This is general skincare information, not medical advice. Individual reactions vary — a comedogenic rating is a guide, not a guarantee.

How the comedogenic scale works

Ingredients are rated from 0 to 5 for how likely they are to clog pores. 0 means non-comedogenic; 5 means highly comedogenic. For oily or acne-prone skin, it's generally worth avoiding anything rated 3 or higher. The scale comes from older lab tests on rabbit ears, not human skin directly, so treat it as a useful guide rather than an absolute rule — concentration, formulation, and your own skin all affect the real-world result.

Coconut oil is the one people are most surprised by, since it's marketed heavily as a "natural" fix. On oily or acne-prone skin, its thick consistency makes it one of the more reliable pore-cloggers on this list.

Generally safe, even for oily and acne-prone skin

Not every oil or emollient behaves the same way. Mineral oil, glycerin, hyaluronic acid, and hemp seed oil all rate at or near 0 and are considered safe starting points even for reactive, oily skin. Squalane (note: squalane with an "a", not squalene) is another one worth knowing — despite being oil-like, it doesn't share the pore-clogging or oxidation issues of many plant oils.

Beyond comedogenicity: ingredients that irritate

A separate issue from clogged pores is irritation — ingredients that don't necessarily block pores but inflame already-reactive skin. The most common offenders are denatured alcohol, synthetic fragrance, and harsh physical exfoliants like crushed shells or nut husks. If your skin reacts to a product but the ingredients aren't rated high on the comedogenic scale, this is often the reason.

Reading the full ingredient list matters more than trusting a "non-comedogenic" label on the front of the bottle — that claim isn't regulated, and a product can carry it while still containing a couple of higher-rated ingredients further down the list.

How to use this in practice

Check new products against this list before buying, especially anything marketed as "natural" or "nourishing" — those words often signal heavier plant oils and butters. If a product breaks you out and none of its ingredients rate high on comedogenicity, look instead at fragrance or alcohol content as the more likely cause.
